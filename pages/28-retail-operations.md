## Copy

**Browser title:** Retail operations — Feld Company

**Meta description:** Allocation and the labor schedule. The store as an operation, not a brand. Truth about inventory before any sentence to a customer.

**Eyebrow:** Industries · Retail operations

**Headline:** Monday's schedule is the product.

**Dek:** We do not design the shopfront or the campaign. We work where allocation meets the room, and where a labor schedule either respects the people on it or pretends to.

### Allocation

What the store was told would arrive, what the distribution center believes, what the back room holds. An agent can draft the exception list: the gap between those three. A planner releases the list that will change a truck. Until the three sources are in the access design, the agent lists nothing, because a confident gap analysis on bad reads is how a floor stops trusting the next tool.

### The schedule

A labor schedule is a promise to people. An agent may draft it from the rules the company already published: hours, skills, the close, the delivery window. It may not invent a rule that makes the week cheaper. A manager releases the schedule. Changes after release are a human act, with the prior version kept.

We will not install a schedule that the people on it cannot see in time to live their lives. "In time" is defined with them during cartography, not by us in a workshop.

### The storefront, left alone

Customer-facing chat, the campaign, the visual story: out of scope for this floor's page, and usually out of scope for the firm. If the letter is about a brand voice, we will point you elsewhere.

### Close

Bring last Monday's schedule and the exception that broke it. The gap is the process.
